You said:
I want to understand sourdough properly. My loaves come out dense and I don't really know why. Where should I start?
ChatGPT said:
Start with what makes bread rise at all. Dough rises because gas gets trapped in a stretchy network of gluten. In sourdough, the gas comes from a starter: a culture of wild yeast and lactic acid bacteria living in flour and water.

So a dense loaf usually means one of three things:
1. Not enough gas was made (the starter was weak, or the dough didn't ferment long enough).
2. The gas escaped (the gluten network was too weak to hold it).
3. The gas was made too early and the structure collapsed (over-proofing).

We can go through each: the starter, fermentation, gluten, and proofing. Which do you want first?
You said:
The starter. What is actually living in there?
ChatGPT said:
Two groups of microbes share the jar.

- **Wild yeasts** (often Saccharomyces cerevisiae or Kazachstania humilis) eat sugars and produce carbon dioxide and a little alcohol. The CO2 is what raises the bread.
- **Lactic acid bacteria** (Lactobacillus and relatives, now split into genera like Fructilactobacillus sanfranciscensis) eat sugars too, but mostly produce lactic and acetic acid. They make the sour flavour, and the acid lowers the pH.

They cooperate: the bacteria can use maltose the yeast ignores, and the acidic environment keeps most other microbes out. A healthy starter doubles in 4 to 8 hours after feeding at about 24 °C.
You said:
What do you mean by feeding? And what does the ratio like 1:5:5 mean?
ChatGPT said:
Feeding means discarding most of the starter and adding fresh flour and water, so the microbes have new food and the acid gets diluted.

A ratio like 1:5:5 is starter : flour : water by weight. 20 g starter, 100 g flour, 100 g water. A bigger ratio (1:10:10) takes longer to peak, which is handy if you feed at night and bake in the morning. A smaller ratio (1:1:1) peaks fast, in 3 to 4 hours when warm.

The "peak" is when the starter has risen the most and is just starting to fall. That's when it has the most active yeast, and it's the best moment to mix it into dough.
You said:
Back up. What is gluten exactly? I keep hearing about it but I don't know what it is.
ChatGPT said:
Gluten isn't in dry flour as such. Wheat flour contains two groups of proteins, **glutenins** and **gliadins**. When you add water and work the dough, they hydrate and bond into a network:

- glutenins form long, springy chains, which give **elasticity** (the dough snaps back);
- gliadins act like a lubricant between them, which gives **extensibility** (the dough stretches without tearing).

That network is gluten. It traps the CO2 bubbles like balloons. Stronger flour (more protein, 12 to 14 percent) makes a stronger network; that's why bread flour behaves differently from all-purpose.
You said:
So how do I make the gluten stronger without a mixer?
ChatGPT said:
Three tools, all hands-off or gentle:

1. **Autolyse**: mix only the flour and water and leave them 30 to 60 minutes before adding starter and salt. Enzymes start breaking starch and the proteins hydrate, so gluten forms on its own.
2. **Stretch and folds**: every 30 minutes during the first two hours of bulk fermentation, grab one side of the dough, stretch it up and fold it over. Four sets of these build as much strength as kneading.
3. **Time**: gluten keeps developing as the dough ferments.

Salt also tightens gluten, which is why some bakers add it after the autolyse.
You said:
What is bulk fermentation? Is it different from proofing?
ChatGPT said:
Yes, they're two stages.

- **Bulk fermentation** is the first rise, of the whole batch of dough, right after mixing. It's where most of the flavour and gas are made. At 24 to 26 °C it takes about 4 to 6 hours with a typical 20 percent starter. You're looking for a 50 to 75 percent rise, a domed surface, and bubbles on the sides.
- **Proofing** (the final proof) happens after you've divided and shaped the dough. The shaped loaf rises again, often in the fridge overnight (a "cold retard") at 3 to 5 °C for 8 to 16 hours.

Most dense loaves are **under-fermented in bulk**. People end bulk by the clock rather than by how the dough looks.
You said:
How do I tell when bulk is done if I can't trust the clock?
ChatGPT said:
Use several signs together:

- **Volume**: use a straight-sided container and mark the starting level. Aim for 50 to 75 percent growth.
- **Surface**: domed, with a few bubbles, and the edges where the dough meets the container curve upwards.
- **Jiggle**: shake the container; well-fermented dough wobbles like set jelly.
- **Feel**: it's airy and lighter, and it holds a shape rather than slumping.

Temperature changes everything. Every 2 °C warmer shortens fermentation by roughly a quarter. A dough thermometer is the cheapest thing that will improve your bread.
You said:
And over-proofing, what does it look like?
ChatGPT said:
An over-proofed loaf has run out of structure: the yeast made gas for so long that the gluten weakened and the acid started breaking it down.

Signs before baking: the dough is slack, spreads when turned out, and a poke leaves a dent that doesn't spring back at all. After baking: a flat loaf, big irregular holes under the crust, and a sour, boozy smell.

The poke test in proofing: flour a finger and press 1 cm in.
- springs back quickly: under-proofed;
- springs back slowly and leaves a small dent: ready;
- doesn't spring back: over-proofed.
You said:
What does the oven do? Why do people bake in a Dutch oven?
ChatGPT said:
In the first 10 to 15 minutes, the loaf has its **oven spring**: the gas expands in the heat, the yeast has a last burst of activity, and water turns to steam. The loaf can grow 20 to 30 percent.

For that to happen the crust has to stay soft a little longer. Steam keeps the surface moist and stretchy. A Dutch oven traps the loaf's own steam, so you get oven spring and a glossy crust without a steam-injection oven. Typical: 250 °C with the lid on for 20 minutes, then 230 °C lid off for 20 to 25 minutes until the crust is deep brown and the inside reaches about 96 °C.

**Scoring** (slashing the top with a blade) gives the loaf a planned weak spot, so it expands there instead of bursting at the side.
You said:
What is hydration and why do recipes say 75%?
ChatGPT said:
Hydration is water weight divided by flour weight. 750 g water to 1000 g flour is 75 percent.

- 65 to 70 percent: easy to handle, tighter crumb; good for learning.
- 75 to 80 percent: more open crumb, stickier dough, needs more strength building.
- over 80 percent: very open, hard to shape.

Count the starter's flour and water too if you want the true figure. If your loaves are dense, lowering hydration to 70 percent while you learn fermentation often helps: a stiffer dough is easier to shape tightly, and shaping builds the surface tension that holds the loaf up.
You said:
OK so if I put it all together, what order should I learn this in?
ChatGPT said:
Here's an order where each step needs the one before:

1. Keep a starter that reliably doubles (feeding, ratios, peak).
2. Understand gluten (the proteins, autolyse, stretch and folds).
3. Read bulk fermentation by sight and temperature, not the clock.
4. Shaping, for surface tension.
5. Proofing and the poke test, including the cold retard.
6. Baking: oven spring, steam, scoring.
7. Then play with hydration and flour types.

Bake the same recipe (say 70 percent hydration, 20 percent starter, 2 percent salt) several times and change one thing each time.
You said:
One more thing: why does whole wheat flour make it even denser?
ChatGPT said:
Whole wheat flour includes the bran and germ. The sharp bran particles cut gluten strands, and bran absorbs a lot of water, so the dough feels stiffer at the same hydration while the gluten is weaker. Whole wheat also ferments faster, because it has more nutrients for the microbes.

To compensate: add 5 to 10 percent more water, let the flour soak (autolyse) longer, and shorten bulk a little. Or start with 20 to 30 percent whole wheat mixed into white bread flour.
